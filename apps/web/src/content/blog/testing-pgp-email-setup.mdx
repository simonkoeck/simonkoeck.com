I set up PGP email this week. The setup itself was painless. Thunderbird generated a keypair for me from its built-in OpenPGP settings, attached an Autocrypt header to my outgoing mail, and my WKD endpoint resolved when I queried it from a fresh shell. Every box I could check, checked. What I had no way to know was whether any of it actually composed into something a stranger could use to encrypt to me.

The standard way to test this is to send yourself a message from another machine. It does not really work. You are also the recipient, so any decrypt-side mistake gets masked by your own setup. You need a real stranger with PGP to try, and that population is small in 2026.

So I wrote a small tool that plays the stranger. You can find it at [mailenc.org](https://mailenc.org). You open the site, copy the address it shows you, and send it a PGP-encrypted email from your normal client. The site reads what you sent, looks for your public key the way a real correspondent would, encrypts a reply, and writes back. If the encrypted reply lands and decrypts cleanly, you know the chain worked. If it does not, the report tells you which piece is broken.

## Why this is harder than it sounds

A working PGP email setup is at least four separate things stacked on top of each other.

The envelope has to be right. PGP/MIME wraps the message in a `multipart/encrypted` container. Inline PGP dumps a `-----BEGIN PGP MESSAGE-----` block into the body. Different clients prefer different ones and some only handle one of the two cleanly.

The encryption has to actually decrypt. The right cipher, a key with the right capabilities, an intact integrity check. Modern OpenPGP has several ways to get this wrong, most of which produce no error on the sender side.

Your public key has to be findable. This is the silent failure mode. You can send perfectly encrypted email forever, but if a stranger has no way to find your key, nobody can encrypt anything back to you.

And if you signed your message, the signature has to verify against whatever key they did find. Which may or may not be the key your client used to sign with.

A round-trip test answers all four at once. There is nothing to fake. The reply either lands and decrypts and validates, or it does not.

## How to publish a PGP key

There are three real options.

WKD (Web Key Directory) is the strongest. The URL is deterministic from your address, the key sits on your own domain under `/.well-known/openpgpkey/`, and the only person who can publish anything there is whoever runs your DNS. That last property is what makes WKD different from everything else. No third parties involved.

Autocrypt is the most pragmatic. Your client attaches your public key as a header on every outbound message. Recipients cache the header, and after a few weeks of mail you correspond with, everyone has your key without doing anything. The downside is that anyone who has ever emailed you could in principle have planted any Autocrypt header. It is useful as "the key I have been seeing for this person", not as a real binding.

HKPS keyservers like keys.openpgp.org are the fallback. Anyone can upload. The modern ones verify the email address before publishing, which is better than nothing, but the assertion is "someone with access to this mailbox uploaded this key", not "this is the canonical key for this address". For a fresh setup it is often the only thing available, so the bot does check it.

The bot tries all three in parallel and prefers them in that order: WKD, then Autocrypt, then HKPS. The report tells you which one it actually used, so if your supposedly correct WKD setup is broken, you can see Autocrypt picked instead and know where to look.

## Try it

If you have just set up PGP, or you have been using it for years and never really stress-tested it, [mailenc.org](https://mailenc.org) takes about thirty seconds. No signup. Encrypted reply lands in your inbox with a list of every check that passed and every one that did not.
